Study · bulk transfer over EtherCAT

10 MB through an ESC on SPI

A PC master moves 10,000,000 bytes to an application behind a LAN9252-class EtherCAT slave controller (4 KiB RAM) on SPI. What limits the transfer, and which changes help, by how much, on what conditions?

simulated

Every number on this page is a simulation result. No lab measurement exists yet. The SPI's effective rate and per-access cost, which matter most, are placeholders until they are measured. Read the results as how the outcome depends on the system, not as predictions. The full argument is in the report.

Where the time goes

Start from the requirement

The first decision is not a parameter: how long may the transfer take, and how much of the link and of the SPI may it use? Set the limits; every simulated configuration below is checked against them.

Paths

1. The master's mailbox scheduling sets the order of magnitude

FoE and CoE are stop-and-wait. A master that serves the mailbox a fixed number of times per cycle moves one mailbox of data every two cycles, whatever the SPI does: about (mailbox − 12 B) / (2 × cycle). More datagrams per cycle do not help with one bulk slave, because the mailbox holds one message. A master that polls in idle bus time is 1.5–12× faster, at the cost of link load.

Table

2. Mailbox size: bigger helps FoE; EoE has a trap

At a fixed service rate FoE time falls in proportion to the mailbox's data size. EoE fragments other than the last carry multiples of 32 bytes, so a 1,514-byte frame needs four fragments with a 512 B mailbox, three with 522 B and two with 778 B. With whole-buffer SPI reads, larger EoE mailboxes can even be slower.

Table

3. Once the master polls freely, the SPI decides

With an idle-time master, transfer time is close to inversely proportional to the SPI's effective rate. Reading only the message (header, then the announced length, then the byte that releases the buffer) instead of the whole SyncManager buffer gains about as much as doubling the rate. Across the plausible range, the same configuration spans more than an order of magnitude: this is why the SPI must be measured first.

Table

4. A process-data channel is fast and fixed-rate, within ESC RAM and SPI limits

A sequence-numbered chunk per cycle in the outputs, cumulative acknowledgements in the inputs, go-back-N on a stall. Its rate is bytes per cycle ÷ cycle time, and the 4 KiB of ESC RAM caps it at 1,260 B per cycle here. If the SPI cannot copy every cycle's chunk (above about 90 % PDI load), chunks are overwritten and throughput collapses erratically.

What to do, in order

  1. Fix the requirement. Moved once (configuration, firmware), periodically, or continuously; which direction; by when. If it must take under about 3 s, or recur often, plan a separate Ethernet path instead of tuning EtherCAT. If tens of seconds are fine, standard FoE with sensible mailbox sizes is enough.
  2. Measure the SPI on the target with the driver that will ship: effective read and write rates and the fixed cost per access, in both directions. See the lab plan.
  3. Capture the real master's mailbox polling during an FoE transfer and open it in FlowLab's Mailbox view: polling cadence, empty reads and rejected writes decide whether the fixed-rate or the SPI-bound results apply.
  4. Calibrate, then re-run the report with the measured values, and check the best candidates in the lab against the baseline and a plain-Ethernet transfer.
